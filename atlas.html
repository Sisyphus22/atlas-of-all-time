      Era guide · the world of Werwulf

      England in the 1200s

      Robert Eggers' Werwulf is set in 13th-century England. It was a country of about four to five million people by 1300 (estimates vary), almost all of them living in villages. Most were peasants, and many were unfree villeins who could not leave their lord's manor without permission.

      Three languages shared one island. Villagers spoke Middle English, lords and the court spoke Anglo-Norman French, and the church and the law courts wrote in Latin. The word werwulf is Old English for 'man-wolf'.

      'Forest' was a legal word, not a description of trees. Royal forest law covered huge areas, at its peak perhaps a third of the country, and taking the king's deer could cost you a hand or your life until the Charter of the Forest in 1217. An outlaw was said to 'bear a wolf's head': anyone could kill him like a wolf. Real wolves were being hunted out; in 1281 Edward I paid a man to destroy them in the western counties.

      Werewolves lived in stories and sermons. Marie de France's Bisclavret made the werewolf the sympathetic hero, Gerald of Wales reported talking wolves in Ireland, and churchmen argued, following Saint Augustine, that a man could not truly change shape, only seem to. The burning of 'werewolves' came later, in 16th-century France and Germany.

      The century ends in trouble: a cooling climate, the Great Famine of 1315, and in 1348 the Black Death.
